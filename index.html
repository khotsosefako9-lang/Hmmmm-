How the model works and what it leaves out

Starting points for 2026 are rounded from recent data: unemployment of about 33.5% (Stats SA, Q2 2026), repo rate of 7.25% (SARB, September 2026), debt near 78% of GDP and Treasury's planned primary surpluses (February 2026 Budget).

Each governing option changes three things. The first is the extra risk premium investors demand on government bonds, which hits confidence, weakens the rand, pushes up inflation and raises the interest bill. The second is government spending relative to today's budget plan, which gives a short-term boost but adds to debt. The third is how much reform on energy, logistics and municipalities gets delivered. That lifts the economy's speed limit slowly, over several years.

Default settings: GNU or similar centre coalition, no extra premium, no extra spending, reform score 50. ANC minority, premium +0.75, spending +0.3% of GDP, reform 30. ANC with MK/EFF, premium +2.0, spending +1.5% of GDP, reform 40. DA-led coalition, premium −0.5, spending −0.3% of GDP, reform 65. Fragmented, premium +1.0, spending +0.5% of GDP, reform 25. The premium numbers reflect how bond markets would most likely react, based on how they have reacted to past political news. Supporters of some of these parties would argue markets overreact.

The model leaves out inequality and who actually gains from growth, the outcomes of land reform or nationalisation, crime, corruption, and shocks other than oil. Those matter a lot when judging any of these options, and they sit at the centre of the case that supporters of more redistributive policies make.
